Abstract
Neural networks that assign scores to concepts governed by a symbolic ontology
face a consistency problem at inference time: a protein may receive a higher predicted
probability of belonging to a specialized cellular-component term than to the broader
term containing it, even though the ontology mandates that any member of the narrower
class must also be a member of the broader one. Weighted model counting (WMC)
addresses this by treating predictor scores as Bernoulli parameters, summing the weights
of all assignments that satisfy the ontology, and renormalizing. The result is a principled
posterior distribution whose support is confined to logically valid labelings, computed
entirely from fixed predictor scores without any retraining.
The computational bottleneck is structural width: exact WMC is exponential in the
treewidth of the constraint graph, and large biomedical ontologies contain thousands
of reconvergent regions where independent messages from different paths double-
count shared evidence, making exact inference intractable for monolithic solvers and
path-wise approximations inaccurate.
We introducemodular separator WMC, a framework that decomposes the constraint
graph into a small core and peripheral modules, eliminates each module into a joint
boundary factor (separator), and contracts a reduced core. Exact separators provably
recover the global WMC posterior. For wide boundaries, we construct nonnegative
tensor-train (TT) separators by querying a boundary-conditioned oracle directly, project
logical support separately, and inject the factorized TT into reduced-core inference
without materializing dense tables. Recursive core contraction handles ontologies
whose reduced core remains intractable at the first level.
We prove that exact separators preserve the monolithic WMC posterior (Proposi-
tion 6.1) and that module marginal error is bounded by boundary posterior error under
TV distance (Proposition 6.2). Experiments on five complete biomedical ontologies—

1. Introduction
Neuro-symbolic prediction tasks pair a neural scorer with a symbolic knowledge base that
restricts which output configurations are valid. In hierarchical multi-label classification, a
classifier assigns independent scores to labels arranged in a taxonomy, but the taxonomy itself
demands that any positive prediction at a specific concept implies positive predictions at all
of its ancestors [Silla and Freitas, 2011]. In protein-function prediction, the Gene Ontology
(GO) encodes is-a relations among hundreds of thousands of molecular-function, biological-
process, and cellular-component terms: a protein predicted to perform a highly specialized
enzymatic activity must also be predicted to perform the general biochemical processes
containing it [Ashburner et al., 2000, The Gene Ontology Consortium, 2023]. More broadly,
ontology-based classification—across anatomy [Mungall et al., 2012], chemistry [Hastings
et al., 2016], phenotype, and disease—faces the same structural issue: a vector of local
predictor scores is inconsistent with probability one, because independent scoring ignores
the implication structure of the ontology. We call any complete assignment satisfying
all ontology constraints a valid labeling; raw predictor scores typically assign nonzero
probability mass to millions of invalid labelings.
Weighted model counting (WMC) provides a principled, training-free mechanism to
correct this inconsistency. Given a Boolean formula T (encoding the ontology constraints)
and nonnegative literal weights (derived from predictor scores), WMC sums the weights of
all satisfying assignments of T [Chavira and Darwiche, 2008]. Interpreting the predictor
output pi ∈ (0, 1) for ontology term i as the Bernoulli parameter of yi ∈ {0, 1}, the product

Despite its conceptual appeal, exact WMC is #P-hard in general [Valiant, 1979], and
practical solvers based on knowledge compilation or variable elimination can require time
and memory exponential in the treewidth of the constraint graph GT [Bodlaender, 1993,
Darwiche and Marquis, 2002, Darwiche, 2011]. State-of-the-art WMC solvers such as
ADDMC, DPMC, and d4 [Dudek et al., 2020a,b, Lagniez and Marquis, 2017] achieve
remarkable scalability on random or industrial instances, but biomedical ontologies expose
a different structural regime: the number of variables is orders of magnitude larger than
in benchmark suites, while the logical structure—is-a implications forming a directed
acyclic graph —is comparatively simple. GO biological process encodes 25,950 terms
with thousands of reconvergent paths where multiple specialized terms share a common
ancestor through different routes. On such inputs, a 300 GB junction-tree backend exhausts
its memory budget before completing even a single ontology.
The cheaper alternative—loopy belief propagation (BP) [Pearl, 1988]—passes messages
along individual paths and merges them independently at each variable. This is computa-
tionally efficient but formally incorrect on reconvergent structures. When two paths from a
common ancestor d arrive at a shared descendant a through intermediates b and c, messages
along d → b → a and d → c → a both carry evidence from d; treating them as independent
double-counts that evidence and changes the posterior defined by global WMC. We make
this failure mode precise in Section 4 with a four-variable example that shows the path-wise
estimate and the exact WMC marginal diverging by more than 8% relative error.
Compositional WMC methods [Dal et al., 2017, 2021] reduce cost by partitioning a
formula into components that communicate through separator variables. These methods
do not address the regime where boundary sizes routinely reach hundreds or thousands of
variables, as observed in GO biological process (max|Bj| = 624) and ChEBI (|Bj| = 1,082)
in our experiments. Dense separator tables are infeasible at these widths. Low-rank tensor
representations have been applied to graphical model inference [Novikov et al., 2014,
Wrigley et al., 2017] and knowledge compilation [Goral et al., 2024, Onaka et al., 2025], but
direct construction of a TT separator from boundary-conditioned oracle queries—without
compiling the full dense table first—is not covered by existing frameworks.
We introduce modular separator WMC to address both requirements. Our framework
proceeds in three stages. Boundary-aware decomposition: a core-selection algorithm
identifies variables C such that the remaining variables partition into modules—connected
components of GT \ C—each with a boundary-constrained width within a prescribed
cap. Module elimination and separator construction: each module Mj is eliminated by
summing out its interior while holding boundary variables Bj fixed, producing a separator
factor Fj(yBj)—a joint function capturing all dependence the module induces on the rest
of the ontology. For wide boundaries, we build a nonnegative tensor-train separator by
querying the factor at sampled boundary assignments and fitting a rank-r TT using alternating
nonnegative least squares; logical support is maintained separately. Core contraction and
marginal recovery: the original normalizer is recovered by contracting the reduced core
with one separator factor per eliminated module. A calibrated module junction tree then
reuses its messages to recover every eliminated variable’s marginal through backward

2. Related Work
2.1. Constrained and Ontology-Based Prediction
Hierarchical multi-label classification (HMC) requires that positive predictions respect
a taxonomic parent-child ordering [Silla and Freitas, 2011]. Methods for enforcing this
constraint range from architecture design [Giunchiglia and Lukasiewicz, 2020] to constraint-
aware losses such as semantic loss [Xu et al., 2018] and SPL [Ahmed et al., 2022], to
posterior regularization [Ganchev et al., 2010] and constrained decoding [Diligenti et al.,
2017]. Correction methods applied at inference time include upward max-closure [Obozinski
et al., 2008] and sum-product belief propagation over the constraint graph [Giunchiglia and
Lukasiewicz, 2020].
In protein-function prediction, methods such as DeepGO [Kulmanov et al., 2018], Deep-
GOZero [Kulmanov and Hoehndorf, 2022], and DeepGOCNN [Kulmanov and Hoehndorf,
2020] assign per-term scores evaluated under the CAFA framework [Radivojac et al., 2013,

===== PDF PAGE 5 =====
Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 5
Zhou et al., 2019]. Post-processing methods—including max-closure and loopy BP—are
applied to improve constraint satisfaction after training. Our work studies WMC as a plug-in
inference layer in this setting, targeting the same fixed-score correction objective while
returning a fully normalized posterior rather than a constraint-satisfying point estimate.
2.2. Exact Weighted Model Counting
Weighted model counting computes the sum of weights of satisfying assignments of a
Boolean formula [Chavira and Darwiche, 2008]. It generalizes model counting (#SAT) to
real-valued weights and underlies probabilistic inference in graphical models, Bayesian
networks, and probabilistic logic programs [Fierens et al., 2015, De Raedt et al., 2007].
Knowledge compilation translates a formula into a tractable circuit representation that
supports WMC in linear time in the circuit size. Representative target languages include
binary decision diagrams (BDDs), deterministic decomposable negation normal form (d-
DNNF) [Darwiche and Marquis, 2002], and sentential decision diagrams (SDDs) [Darwiche,
2011]. Variable elimination approaches such as ADDMC [Dudek et al., 2020a], DPMC
[Dudek et al., 2020b], and sharpSAT-TD process the formula directly, avoiding a separate
compilation step. The d4 compiler [Lagniez and Marquis, 2017] interleaves clause learning
with compilation and performs competitively on industrial instances. These solvers are
evaluated annually at the Model Counting Competition [Fichte et al., 2021]; we include
ADDMC, DPMC, and d4 in our experimental comparison (Section 7.4).
All of these methods treat the full variable set monolithically: they do not exploit
ontology-specific structure (sparse DAG, large scale, predictor-valued weights) to partition
computation. The regime targeted by ontology-conditioned WMC—hundreds of thousands
of variables, moderately simple logical structure, real-valued weights changing at each
inference call—is distinct from the small-to-medium Boolean instances that dominate
benchmark suites.
2.3. Compositional and Modular Model Counting
Compositional WMC decomposes a formula into independent or loosely coupled compo-
nents and recombines their counts, reducing cost when the formula is nearly disconnected
[Dal et al., 2017, 2021]. Dal et al. [2021] generalize this to approximate probabilistic
inference in Bayesian networks, communicating between components through separator
variables with a product approximation. Ontology modularity studies logically meaningful
decompositions based on the import closure or atomic decomposition of a description logic
ontology [Cuenca Grau et al., 2008, Del Vescovo et al., 2011]; these decompositions are
defined at the ontology level and do not account for predictor-weighted inference.
Our framework specializes compositional WMC to predictor-weighted ontology theories.
Three aspects distinguish it from prior compositional approaches. First, the module decom-
position is driven by the structural width of the constraint graph under the given predictor
weights—the core-selection algorithm responds to the elimination order induced by the
weighted theory at inference time. Second, our separator factors are joint over all boundary
variables, retaining the full dependence that the eliminated module induces on the rest of the
ontology; prior compositional approaches use factored or independence approximations at

oracle queries and TT fitting—a regime ( |Bj| in the hundreds to thousands) not addressed
by existing compositional methods. Dal et al. [2021] explicitly note that their approach is
limited to small separators.
2.4. Tensor-Based Inference and Model Counting
Low-rank tensor representations support efficient operations on high-dimensional probability
distributions. Tensor-train (TT) decompositions [Oseledets, 2011] and cross-approximation
algorithms [Oseledets and Tyrtyshnikov, 2010] provide compact representations of functions
over exponentially many inputs. In graphical models, tensor-network contractions have been
used for marginal inference [Novikov et al., 2014] and as an alternative to belief propagation
[Wrigley et al., 2017]. Ducamp et al. [2020] apply low-rank decompositions to potential
functions in Bayesian networks.
In model counting, Goral et al. [2024] introduce semiring extensions that encompass
WMC and weighted counting variants, establishing algebraic foundations for combining
tensor operations with counting. Onaka et al. [2025] directly compare tensor-train representa-
tions with OBDDs for knowledge compilation, finding that TTs achieve competitive size on
certain formula families. These works compile a static formula into a TT or tensor-network
representation. Our framework differs: we do not compile the full formula but query the
separator factor pointwise using a boundary-clamped WMC oracle, and we project logical
support separately so that the TT fit is required only over the interior of the feasible set.
The use of nonnegative TT decompositions with alternating least-squares fitting [Lee and
Seung, 2001] ensures that the separator factor represents a nonnegative function, consistent
with its interpretation as an unnormalized marginal likelihood. This nonnegativity constraint,
absent in standard TT-SVD decompositions, is important for preserving the probabilistic
semantics of the WMC posterior.
2.5. Message Passing and Approximate Inference
Belief propagation (BP) [Pearl, 1988] is exact on trees and provides an efficient but ap-
proximate alternative to junction-tree inference on graphs with cycles. On ontology graphs,
loopy BP passes messages along is-a edges, but reconvergent paths cause double-counting
of shared evidence; the resulting fixed-point marginals differ from the globally normalized
WMC posterior (Section 4). Damped and synchronous BP variants [Murphy et al., 1999]
improve convergence but do not correct the fundamental reconvergence error. Our frame-
work retains exact message passing where the structure permits (inside each module and
over the reduced core) while using joint separator factors to handle reconvergent interfaces
between modules.
Expectation propagation [Minka, 2001] and variational methods [Wainwright and Jordan,
2008] provide alternative approximation schemes but do not directly minimize the error in
the WMC normalizer. For fixed predictor scores and hard logical constraints, the WMC
posterior is the unique target, and any approximation is measured by its deviation from that
posterior—the criterion tracked by our TV-distance stability bound (Proposition 6.2).

5. Modular Separator WMC
5.1. Overview
Given the core, modules, and boundaries defined above, the method proceeds in three steps:
(1) eliminate each module while retaining its boundary, producing one joint separator factor;
(2) contract the reduced core with all separator factors to recover core marginals and joint
boundary posteriors; (3) recover module-interior marginals through backward calibration of
saved module junction trees. Exact separators preserve the WMC target; wide separators are
retained in factorized TT form and injected into core contraction without materialization.

marginal error of any single label satisfies|pT (yi = 1)−bp(yi = 1)| ≤ TV(bp(yMj), pT (yMj)),
the single-label bound follows.
Proposition 6.2 is independent of how the boundary posterior is computed. Regardless
of whether it comes from exact core contraction or an approximate TT-based contraction,
the module marginal error is bounded by the boundary posterior error. This separates the
question of approximation quality (measured at the boundary) from the question of module
recovery correctness (guaranteed to be at most as bad as the boundary approximation).
The kernel-verified Lean 4 development in Appendix G formally checks the diamond
identities (5/6 vs. 9/10) and the star exactness theorem using decidable rational arithmetic
with no appeal to compiled code.

8. Discussion
8.1. Why Biomedical Ontologies Are Amenable to Modular Decomposition
The success of modular separator WMC on GO, UBERON, and ChEBI rests on a structural
property of these ontologies: despite their large size and high treewidth in the monolithic
graph, a small fraction of high-degree variables (the core) acts as a separator that isolates
the remaining terms into many small, tractable peripheral modules. Table 8 shows that cores
of 2–9% of all terms suffice to produce modules covering 91–100% of terms at boundary-
last widths of 5–12. This tree-like periphery with a narrow bottleneck is characteristic of
biomedical ontologies built around hierarchical subsumption: most terms have few parents,

===== PDF PAGE 18 =====
Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 18
and reconvergence is concentrated at a small number of high-degree terms.
This structural property is not shared by all knowledge bases. Ontologies with dense
cross-concept constraints (e.g., disjointness or covering axioms at scale) may produce larger
cores and wider boundaries, reducing the benefit of modular decomposition. Section 5.8
already handles wide boundaries through TT separators; the limits of this approach are
explored in Appendix ??.
8.2. The Role of Joint Boundary Factors
A key design choice is the use of joint separator factors Fj(yBj) rather than factored approx-
imations. The reconvergence analysis in Section 4 shows why factored approximations fail:
when multiple paths from a common ancestor converge at a shared boundary variable, the
joint distribution over boundary variables encodes the non-independence induced by the
eliminated module. Table 4 confirms empirically that the product baseline (which uses a
factored separator) consistently produces substantially higher errors than TT-2, even though
both respect the logical support.
8.3. Limitations
GO cc degeneracy. The current experimental design validates exactness on GO cc (Ta-
ble 1) but the GO cc decomposition reduces to monolithic JT. We address this by pointing
to the GO bp exact-reference modules in Table 4, which show machine-precision recovery
on genuine modular instances. Future work should produce a standalone table of exactness
results for GO mf and GO bp subgraphs with depth≥ 1 decompositions, as outlined in the
experiment design of Appendix A.
Approximate separators lack a posteriori certificates. Proposition 6.2 bounds module
marginal error by boundary posterior error, but does not provide a computable certificate for
TV(bp(yBj), pT (yBj)) when exact references are unavailable. Rank self-consistency (Table 3)
provides indirect evidence that rank 8 is near saturation; but for safety-critical applications,
certified error bounds would be needed.
Scalability at rank > 8. For ChEBI with max |Bj| = 1,082, rank-8 TT separators achieve
median validation NMAE 9.7 × 10−3, which may be insufficient for tasks requiring high
marginal accuracy. Higher ranks are feasible in principle but increase bond-state cost in
reduced-core inference. Adaptive rank selection and hierarchical TT representations are
directions for future work.
Coverage. Table 2 reports structural coverage of 91–100%: between 0–9% of terms lie
in the core rather than in peripheral modules. Core-variable marginals are obtained from
reduced-core inference and are exact (or as accurate as the TT separator quality). The
remaining terms’ marginals are within the coverage fraction.

8.4. Future Directions
Downstream evaluation on GO mf and GO bp. The CAFA downstream experiment
(Section 7.5) is on GO cc, where exact JT inference is feasible. Extending this evaluation to
GO mf and GO bp—where TT-mode modular WMC is the only tractable approach—would
directly demonstrate the practical value of the wide-separator construction.
Adaptive decomposition and rank selection. The current core-selection algorithm uses
static degree scores. Score-dependent decomposition (adapting the core to the predictor score
vector at each inference call) could improve module tractability. Similarly, per-module rank
selection based on boundary structure could reduce TT cost without sacrificing accuracy.
Extensions to richer ontology languages. The framework currently handles is-a implica-
tions (Horn 2-clauses). Extensions to OWL DL axioms (covering, disjointness, existential
restrictions) would require handling non-monotone clauses in the module factor graph,
changing the oracle interface and TT support structure.
9. Conclusion
We introduced modular separator WMC, a framework for scaling ontology-conditioned
weighted model counting to large biomedical ontologies. The core technical contributions
are: (i) a boundary-aware core-selection algorithm that decomposes the constraint graph
into a small core and tractable peripheral modules; (ii) exact and nonnegative TT separator
constructions that summarize each module’s effect on the rest of the ontology through a
joint boundary factor; and (iii) backward calibration that recovers all module marginals from
saved junction-tree messages.
Two propositions ground the framework theoretically. Exact separators provably recover
the monolithic WMC posterior, and module marginal error is bounded by boundary posterior
error under total-variation distance. These guarantees hold regardless of how the boundary
posterior is computed, separating the approximation question from the recovery correctness
question.
Experiments on five complete biomedical ontologies show that decomposition enables
all-term inference beyond the 300 GB memory limit of monolithic junction-tree inference,
that state-of-the-art WMC solvers exhaust memory on the same inputs, and that rank-
controlled TT separators systematically improve accuracy as rank increases. On the CAFA
benchmark, exact posterior correction eliminates true-path violations and improves cal-
ibration over raw predictor scores and loopy belief propagation. The results show that
ontology structure—sparse DAG with a small high-degree core and many tractable periphery
modules—is precisely the regime where modular decomposition and joint separator factors
are most effective.

B. Reproducibility Details
The operational selector uses Algorithm 1 with wmax = 14, β = 0.01, and ρ = 0.30. Direct
TT fitting uses r ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8}, 5,000 unique training queries, six alternating-least-squares
sweeps, nonnegative least-squares core updates, and an independent validation set. Scores
in TT mode are clipped to [10−6, 1 − 10−6]. Loopy BP uses synchronous sum-product with
damping 0.5, tolerance 10−9, and at most 20,000 iterations. Exact-reference candidates
retain 108 subgraphs per ontology where brute-force and JT references agree under fixed
random seeds.
Compute resources. CPU-only SLURM runs. Multi-ontology screening uses 16 CPU
cores, 128 GB allocated memory. The full ChEBI recursive run uses 1 CPU core, 400 GB
allocated memory. Monolithic JT rows use a common 300 GB cap.
Data availability. Source code, ontology preprocessing, exact and approximate inference
backends, Lean developments, environment files, seeds, raw results, and table-generation
scripts are included in the submission artifact.

G. Formalisation: Lean 4 Development
The Lean 4 development ( Wmc.lean, Lean 4.31) is a single Lean-core file with no de-
pendence on mathlib. It defines worlds as Boolean lists, the true-path constraint as a
conjunction of edge clauses (¬yc ∨ yπ), and closes every claim by decide using kernel-
checkable rational arithmetic. Verified lemmas include diamond_exact_5_6 (5/6),
diamond_softor_9_10 (9/10), and diamond_softor_ne_exact (inequality).
The parametric star theorem is proved in a separate mathlib development with axiom
footprint {propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound}.
